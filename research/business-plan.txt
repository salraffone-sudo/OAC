1. Executive Summary
1.1 The Promise
OAC is for real estate owners, architects, and contractors. If you read OAC, you will spot collaborative opportunities between the disciplines and better navigate the complexities of your own.
This promise is the editorial north star for every piece of content the platform produces. It is not a tentative offer of awareness—it is an assertive claim about what regular readership delivers: a working lens for seeing your project differently, not just a passive education.
1.2 The Opportunity
The real estate development industry operates at the intersection of three disciplines—ownership/development, architecture/design, and construction/delivery. Professionals in each discipline routinely make decisions that affect the others, yet rarely possess deep working knowledge across all three. This creates persistent friction: miscommunication, misaligned incentives, cost overruns, and missed opportunities.
OAC is a content, tools, and professional services platform built by a practitioner who has held leadership roles across all three disciplines. The name references the Owner-Architect-Contractor coordination meeting—a weekly ritual on every project—and signals to industry professionals that this platform speaks their language.
1.3 The Audience
OAC readers belong to one of the three disciplines, but what unites them is not job title—it is a shared recognition that the system connecting their work is nearly broken and ripe for change. Each discipline experiences this dysfunction differently:
Owners are frustrated by rising costs that force constant quality reduction or push them toward vertical integration just to retain control over outcomes.
Architects are frustrated by escalating code complexity layered on top of owners who pressure them to reduce quality, compress fees, and cut the design experience down to the bone.
Contractors are navigating a never-ending maze of procurement delays, scoping complexity, logistics hurdles, and cost increases, often with the least control over decisions made upstream.
Each group cares about slightly different symptoms of the same underlying disease. The OAC thesis is that good collaboration across the three disciplines can resolve much of what each group experiences as someone else’s problem. The platform exists to make that collaboration legible, practical, and ultimately advisable.
1.4 Business Model Summary
OAC operates as a hybrid model with four product tracks: a tiered content business (free articles building into digests, box sets, and books), a low-cost paid engagement tier (Q&amp;A, symposia, and conversation), a suite of paid professional tools (AI-powered workflow applications mapped to OAC disciplines), and professional services (advisory, architecture, investment). The content explains the thinking. The engagement tier sustains the network. The tools apply the thinking. The advisory services deliver it. Together they function as a self-reinforcing flywheel—detailed fully in Section 2—that generates lead flow for high-value advisory services, architecture commissions, and real estate investment opportunities.
1.5 Core Differentiator
The founder has significant professional experience across all three OAC disciplines—a combination that is exceptionally rare in any one of them. This cross-disciplinary background enables generalist insights that specialists cannot replicate, drawing from lived experience in the gaps and overlaps of the Venn diagram rather than theorizing from the outside. The tools extend this differentiator from insight into action: they encode cross-disciplinary expertise into practical, immediately usable applications.
2. The OAC Flywheel
Every product, vertical, and workflow described in this plan is a gear in a single compounding mechanism. This section states that mechanism explicitly, names what compounds and why, and identifies the risks that could stop it from spinning.
2.1 The Loop
The flywheel is circular, not linear. Each stage feeds the next, and the final stage feeds back into the first:
Written content (free, constant) builds the foundation and establishes credibility.
The paid engagement tier (low-cost, always running) layers on top of the free content, sustaining a network of serious, self-selected readers.
Products, tools, and coursework are built from that foundation—either repackaged from existing content or purpose-built from advisory needs.
Speaking engagements and the products themselves expand the professional network.
The network produces advisory fees, architecture commissions, and investment opportunities.
Advisory work generates new lived experience—which becomes the credible raw material for the next round of written content.
Advisory is not the destination of the flywheel. It is a re-entry point. Content is always borne out of actual experience, which is precisely why it carries credibility that purely theoretical or aggregated content cannot match.
2.2 Why Writing Is the Foundation and the Accelerant
None of the other gears can exist without the constant presence of the writing. Tools, courses, books, and speaking topics all originate from or are validated by what has already been written. Writing is also the slowest gear to show returns and the one that compounds most consistently and enduringly—which is why it must be treated as inviolable rather than flexible. Flywheels are notoriously slow to start: growth begins flat and only becomes vertical after sustained rotation. This is expected, not a warning sign, and the founder is positioned for the long haul accordingly.
In practice, the writing cadence already runs with a working buffer of content scheduled a month or two into the future. This is no longer speculative—it is an established operating rhythm. Other workstreams (tool development, course design, digest compilation) flex around the writing schedule; the writing schedule does not flex around them.
2.3 The Compounding Asset: The Topic Bank
Reputation compounds externally and socially—speaking invitations, course opportunities, and inbound interest get cheaper to win as credibility accumulates. But there is a second, internal compounding asset that explains the mechanism more precisely: the topic bank.
The topic bank does not grow linearly. It grows combinatorially. Every new entry can potentially connect to every prior entry through the OAC zone structure, and as the bank matures, new categories emerge organically from the accumulated material. At 50 entries, there are a few dozen plausible cross-references. At 300 entries, there are thousands. This is the structural reason later articles become easier to find an angle for and richer to write—not simply a vague effect of growing reputation, but a concrete, accumulating network of internal connections.
Reputation and the topic bank compound through different mechanisms—one external and social, one internal and structural—and both should be tracked as distinct flywheel assets rather than conflated into a single notion of “momentum.”
2.4 The Paid Engagement Tier as a Flywheel Accelerant
The paid engagement tier (weekly written Q&amp;A, monthly live Q&amp;A, quarterly symposia, collaborative interviews and conversations) is deliberately kept at a low-cost threshold. It is not designed as a primary revenue source. It is designed to be always humming—a constant, low-friction mechanism that keeps the network of serious readers engaged between major content releases, generates ideas and cross-references that feed back into the free tier, and surfaces the readers most likely to become advisory clients, co-authors, or referral sources.
This design is also a direct response to Substack’s own platform dynamics, and must adapt alongside the platform it relies upon. Paid subscriptions earn subscriber badges, which factor into platform visibility mechanics such as rising lists, and create eligibility for sponsorship and other platform-level opportunities. The low price is intentional: the tier exists to leverage these platform dynamics and spin the flywheel faster, not to maximize subscription revenue. If the tier drifts toward revenue-maximizing pricing or friction, it stops functioning as a network-sustaining gear and becomes a wall between the founder and the people most likely to become advisory clients.
2.5 Advisory as Regional Expansion
Advisory engagements won through network opportunities serve a second strategic function beyond fee revenue: they are a low-cost way to build a regional presence the founder does not otherwise have. Advisory work in a new region adds geographic diversity to lived experience, which increases the footprint and speed of the flywheel by diversifying the raw material that feeds back into content. A flywheel that only draws on experience from one region or one type of project will compound more slowly than one that actively seeks regional and project-type diversity through its advisory work.
2.6 Named Risks to the Flywheel
Two risks could stop the flywheel from spinning. Naming them explicitly is what makes this analysis useful rather than aspirational. Their current ease of execution should not be confused with lesser importance—the whole structure breaks down if either is ignored, even though both are, in practice, becoming integrated into the weekly schedule without significant strain.
Writing cadence or quality drop: Because every other gear depends on the writing, a sustained drop in cadence or quality stalls the entire mechanism, not just the content track. This is the single point of failure for the flywheel.
Paid tier mission drift: If the engagement tier’s low-cost, always-humming discipline slips—if it starts chasing revenue, raises prices, or adds friction—it stops functioning as a network-sustaining gear and becomes a barrier between the founder and the people most likely to become advisory clients.
[OPERATING DISCIPLINE: Both risks are currently well-managed in practice. The note here is to remain vigilant as the business scales, not because either risk is presently acute.]
2.7 Time Allocation Across the Flywheel
Written content is the most significant and most constant time demand, and it operates within the founder’s established capacity—this is no longer speculative. The paid engagement tier layers on top of the writing schedule, borrowing topics and resources from the free tier rather than requiring separate ideation.
All other content and products are created in one of two ways:
Repackaged from existing content: Digests, box sets, and books are built from the article archive. The marginal effort is curation and framing, not new creation.
Purpose-built from advisory needs: Tools, guidelines, and checklists are built to serve a specific need encountered in the advisory business, then repackaged as standalone products.
This two-path structure is what keeps the flywheel sustainable for a solo operator: nothing is created from a blank page except the writing itself. Everything else is either compiled from what already exists or extracted from real advisory work already underway.

3.2 Content Development Process
Article Creation
The founder writes all content, using AI as an interview and ideation partner. AI conducts structured interviews based on the founder’s experience across the three disciplines, generating raw material that the founder refines into publishable articles. Each article focuses on a specific aspect of the OAC Venn diagram—an overlap, a gap, a friction point, or an insight that emerges from cross-disciplinary fluency.
Analytics-Driven Compilation
At every tier, analytics guide compilation decisions. Substack engagement data identifies which topics resonate most. Popular articles seed spin-off articles that expand on high-performing themes. Digests are compiled around themes validated by readership data. Box sets and books are organized around broader patterns that emerge from the analytics.
Upward Mobility of Content
Articles → Digests: Most popular articles grouped by theme; framing articles written to create narrative coherence
Digests → Box Sets: Related digests bundled; exclusive digest written to expand on unifying theme
Box Sets → Books: AI-assisted interview process synthesizes multiple themes into larger thesis; not a simple compilation but a reframing
Monthly Reading List
A byproduct of the weekly scan, not additional work. The scan already curates 10–15 headlines and 2–3 deep dives per week. A monthly reading list compiles the most notable items into a curated resource—a “what to read this month in the OAC world” roundup published as a free Substack post, following the model established by Brian Potter’s Construction Physics newsletter.
The reading list provides additional value at zero marginal writing effort, positions the founder as a knowledgeable curator (reinforcing cross-disciplinary credibility), and creates natural opportunities for Substack cross-promotion.
3.3 The Engagement Tier in Practice
The engagement tier sits structurally between the free Introductory Offer and the paid Attraction Offer, but it is not a content product—it is a relationship product. Its components:
Weekly written Q&amp;A: Paid subscribers submit questions; the founder answers in writing, drawing on real OAC experience. Light lift, high relationship value.
Monthly live Q&amp;A: A scheduled live session (video or audio) for deeper, real-time conversation.
Quarterly symposia: A more substantial live gathering—potentially multi-person—that functions as both a content event and a networking opportunity for paid subscribers.
Collaborative interviews and conversations: Founder-initiated conversations with other practitioners or paid subscribers, which may themselves become free content (with permission) or remain a subscriber-only benefit.
Pricing for this tier should remain low enough that it reads as a modest, easy yes—not as a meaningful purchase decision. Its value is measured by network health and advisory lead quality, not by revenue per subscriber.

4. Content Generation Workflow
Consistent, high-quality content at a pace of two articles per week—while working full-time—requires a systematic intake and ideation process. This section describes three analytical layers operating at different cadences: the automated weekly scan (what to write about), the monthly strategic debrief (how to build the business), and the quarterly craft analysis (how to write effectively). Together with the topic bank and the qualitative feedback loop, these form the content generation engine.
4.1 Weekly Scan: Overview
A scheduled Claude Cowork task runs weekly to scour industry sources, analyze audience engagement data, and deliver a curated briefing. The scan is governed by a prompt file (scan-prompt.md) that defines sources, format, and logic. The output feeds a persistent topic bank (topic-bank.md). Both files are designed to be modified as the process evolves.
4.2 Scan Process: Four Phases
Phase 1: Mood Check
Before scanning, Cowork asks two questions:
Discipline focus: Which part of the OAC map are you drawn to this week? Options: Owner, Architect, Contractor, any pairwise intersection, the center, or “surprise me” (randomized for diversity).
Mode: Reactive (“show me what’s broken, controversial, or misunderstood”) or generative (“feed me something emerging, novel, or inspiring”).
The mood check ensures diversity of input over time. Weeks tagged “surprise me” serve as deliberate disruption.
Phase 2: Industry Scan
Cowork searches across defined source categories, filtered by mood check and weighted toward the selected discipline:
Trade publications: ENR, Architectural Record, Building Design + Construction, and similar
Industry organizations: AIA, ULI, NAIOP, AGC publications, newsletters, and event recaps
The scan cross-references results against the existing topic bank and flags overlaps. Overlapping results are marked, not excluded.
Phase 3: Performance Review (Quantitative + Qualitative Feedback Loop)
Each week, Cowork reviews cumulative engagement data, now expanded to include both quantitative metrics and qualitative signal:
Substack metrics: Open rates, likes, comments, shares, subscriber growth attributed to specific articles
LinkedIn metrics: Impressions, engagement rate, comments, reshares
Restacks: Substack restacks of the founder’s posts are tracked as a distinct signal from likes or comments. Restacks indicate a reader found the piece valuable enough to actively re-share to their own network—a stronger endorsement than passive engagement. Frequency and source of restacks help identify which pieces resonate widely enough to warrant direct spin-offs.
Comment synthesis: Reader comments (on Substack and LinkedIn) are read and synthesized, not just counted. The goal is to discern *why* a piece resonated—which specific claim, framing, or example readers responded to—not just that it did. This is qualitatively distinct from the restack signal: restacks tell you what resonates, comment synthesis tells you why.
Using all-time cumulative data, the performance review identifies the strongest-performing content and does three things with it:
Spin-off suggestions: Breaks popular topics into narrower sub-questions or deeper investigations, informed by both restack patterns and comment themes.
Adjacent topic discovery: Identifies related subject matter the audience would likely respond to based on demonstrated interest patterns.
Voice calibration: Comment synthesis over time reveals which tones, examples, and framings the audience consistently responds to, refining how future articles are written, not just what they cover.
Phase 3a: Analytics Data Access
Substack and LinkedIn do not currently offer public APIs for analytics, and comment and restack data must likewise be reviewed manually or semi-manually. The founder periodically exports or pastes metrics and notable comment themes into analytics-data.md. If programmatic access becomes available, the manual step can be eliminated. The prompt file includes a conditional: if analytics-data.md is stale (&gt;14 days), the performance review is skipped.
Phase 4: Weekly Output
Cowork delivers a single document with four sections:
Deep Dives (2–3 topics): Neutral trend summary plus suggested OAC angle. Framed according to mood—reactive surfaces tensions; generative surfaces opportunities.
Headline Scan (10–15 items): One-line summaries tagged by OAC discipline. Scannable in under 5 minutes.
Performance Review: Top-performing content with spin-off, adjacency, and voice-calibration suggestions, drawing on restack and comment data. Omitted if data is stale.
Topic Bank Updates: Suggested new entries, pre-formatted, with overlap flags.
The full output is designed to be read and acted on in under 30 minutes.
4.3 Quarterly Craft Analysis
While the weekly scan addresses what to write about, the quarterly craft analysis addresses how to write it. A separate Cowork task examines structural and stylistic patterns that drive engagement—in the founder’s content and across a peer set. Adapted from Jenny Ouyang’s research on content performance.
Three-Pass Methodology
Pass 1 — Format Mix: Evaluates distribution of content formats and their relative performance. Looks for high-performing archetypes, especially the “tactical revelation” format: specific claim, immediately usable insight, complete without a click, short enough to remember.
Pass 2 — Structure: Examines internal architecture of posts. What does the first line do? Is value in the text or behind a click? Post-link notes consistently underperform standalone notes.
Pass 3 — Patterns: Identifies recurring patterns and data that contradicts conventional advice, cross-referenced against the comment synthesis from the weekly performance review. Produces an evolving pattern taxonomy:
Tactical Revelation: A specific, actionable claim delivered completely within the post.
Named Behavior: Identifies and names a phenomenon the audience recognizes but has never had language for.
Worldview Statement: A declarative position that invites agreement or productive disagreement.
Milestone Done Right: A milestone shared with craft and substance rather than empty celebration.
Post Link Done Right: When linking is necessary, the post contains enough standalone value that the link is a bonus.
Format Pattern Tagging
Every published article and LinkedIn post is tagged with its primary format pattern in the topic bank, creating a multi-dimensional dataset: what (OAC zone), how (format pattern), and how it performed (engagement data, restacks, and comment themes). The taxonomy evolves as the quarterly analysis identifies new patterns.
Peer Set
A curated set of peer accounts maintained in peer-list.md. Not limited to AEC—effective content craft transcends subject matter. The list grows organically as the founder’s reading habits develop.
Core Principle
The posts that engage a community win. They are not text wrapped around a link—they deliver value on their own. Consistent voice raises your floor permanently; virality raises it once.
4.4 Monthly Strategic Debrief
The monthly debrief addresses how to build the business. A structured reading-and-reflection practice where the founder reads Substack authors and content entrepreneurs solving similar problems—people like Timo Mason (product ecosystem stacking), Jenny Ouyang (engagement mechanics), and Dheeraj Sharma (AI team architecture)—and extracts applicable insights through an AI-assisted interview.
Claude asks: What did this author do that’s applicable to OAC? What’s the gap between their model and yours? What would you adopt or reject? What experiment could you run in the next 30 days? The output is captured in strategy-notes.md, organized by business theme: packaging, pricing, distribution, content strategy, business model evolution.
Monthly is the target cadence but this is the most flexible layer. If nothing grabbed your attention, skip it. If something provocative appears, don’t wait.

4.8 Pre-Publication SEO Optimization
Every article passes through a custom Claude skill before publishing. The skill evaluates the draft against Substack-specific SEO best practices and returns a checklist—it does not rewrite content or compromise voice.
What It Evaluates
SEO Title: Length (40–60 chars), keyword placement, specificity.
Meta Description: Whether opening lines work as search snippet.
URL Slug: Short, keyword-focused (3–5 words), main keyword first.
Header Structure: H2/H3 hierarchy for crawlability.
Internal Links: Cross-references topic bank and published index. The OAC Venn diagram creates natural topical clusters.
Image Alt Text: Flags missing alt text. Suggests accessible, keyword-relevant descriptions.
Keyword Coherence: Core terms align with what AEC professionals actually search for. No keyword stuffing.
Social Preview Image: Confirms image is set (posts with images get significantly more signups and clicks).
The skill adds 5–10 minutes per article. Built after the first 5–10 articles (~month 1–2), refined as search performance data becomes available through Google Search Console.

4.9 AI Team Structure
The content generation workflow is executed by a team of five specialized AI assistants with quality control gating between stages. Adapted from Dheeraj Sharma’s multi-agent content architecture.
The Five Roles
Role 1 — The Scanner: Executes the weekly scan. Goes wide across many topics. Scheduled Cowork task governed by scan-prompt.md.
Role 2 — The Researcher: Activated when the founder selects a topic. Goes deep on one subject—finding current data, competing perspectives, OAC angles not yet explored.
Role 3 — The Interviewer: The writing partner. Conducts the AI-assisted interview that extracts the founder’s cross-disciplinary thinking. The Researcher’s output feeds the Interviewer’s questions.
Role 4 — The Editor: Reviews drafts for coherence, accuracy, voice consistency, and whether the article delivers on the OAC promise stated in Section 1.1. The quality gate that protects the brand.
Role 5 — The Optimizer: The SEO skill plus format pattern validation. The final gate before publishing.
QC Gating
Gate 1 (Scanner → Researcher): Founder selects topic. Only shortlisted topics advance.
Gate 2 (Researcher → Interviewer): Founder reviews research. Insufficient topics return to bank.
Gate 3 (Interviewer → Editor): Raw transcript refined into draft. Founder self-edits first.
Gate 4 (Editor → Optimizer): Editor feedback incorporated. Draft finalized.
Gate 5 (Optimizer → Publish): SEO and format recommendations applied selectively.
The founder is the human in the loop at every gate. No content publishes without explicit approval.
Implementation Path
Phase 1 (Launch): Cowork for Scanner. Claude Projects with system prompts for Researcher, Interviewer, Editor, Optimizer. ~80% capability with minimal setup.
Phase 2 (Month 3–6): Custom Claude skills for Optimizer. Explore Claude Code/MCP architecture.
Phase 3 (Month 6+): If justified, migrate to Claude Code subagents with MCP servers. Agent files are plain English markdown—accessible to non-developers.
5. Builder Component: OAC Tools
The OAC platform includes a suite of paid, AI-powered professional tools that extend the content thesis into practical application. Articles explain the thinking. Tools apply it. As established in Section 2.7, tools are one of the two ways non-writing products are created—either repackaged from existing content or purpose-built from advisory needs and then repackaged. This content-to-tools pipeline creates a flywheel gear that no comparable AEC content platform offers: a reader recognizes a problem in an article, then immediately has a tool to address it.
5.1 Strategic Rationale
The tools serve different strategic purposes depending on the tool:
Lead generation and credibility: Some tools (like an RFP template generator) demonstrate cross-disciplinary expertise to potential advisory clients. The tool itself signals that the founder has operational depth, not just opinions.
Independent revenue: All tools are priced as standalone paid products with real value independent of the content. They generate revenue from users who may never read the Substack.
Advisory differentiator: Tools built for the founder’s own advisory practice become part of the advisory offering. Clients get access to purpose-built tools that competitors don’t have.
Content amplifier: Each tool naturally generates article topics. Building a ROM estimator surfaces insights about how owners, architects, and contractors think about costs differently—which becomes an article, which links back to the tool.
The tools are the only product in the OAC ecosystem that does something for the user rather than teaches them something. This makes them a fundamentally different value proposition from content—and a powerful complement to it.

Best for professionals who already use Claude and want tools that integrate into their existing AI workflow.
Web Application
A standalone web app accessible to anyone with a browser. Simpler to use, no Claude subscription required, but less customizable. Best for professionals who want a point solution without adopting a new platform. Built as lightweight React or HTML applications.
Offering both formats is a competitive advantage. Most AEC tool builders force users into a single platform. OAC lets the buyer decide how they want to work.
5.4 Pricing
All tools are sold as one-time purchases. No subscriptions, no recurring fees. This aligns with the founder’s operational reality as a side project—subscription models require ongoing maintenance, customer support infrastructure, and feature updates that a solo operator cannot reliably sustain.
Simple tools (templates, generators): $25–$75
Mid-complexity tools (evaluators, screeners): $75–$200
High-complexity tools (ROM estimator, coordination checkup): $200–$500

5.5 Content-to-Tools Pipeline
The connection between articles and tools is bidirectional and continuous:
Articles → Tools
Articles explain the frameworks, diagnostic methods, and cross-disciplinary insights that underpin each tool. A reader who understands scope triangulation from the articles sees the Document Completion Evaluator as a natural next step. Articles include links to relevant tools where appropriate—not as upsells but as practical applications of the ideas discussed.
Tools → Articles
Building tools generates article ideas. The process of encoding expertise into a tool surfaces edge cases, exceptions, and nuances that become articles themselves. A tool for ROM cost estimation raises questions about how different delivery methods affect early-stage budgeting—which becomes an article in the O-C zone, which links back to the tool.
Tools → Advisory
Tools built for the founder’s own advisory practice become differentiators in advisory engagements. Clients don’t just get advice—they get access to purpose-built analytical tools. Over time, the tool suite becomes a moat around the advisory practice that competitors cannot easily replicate.
5.6 Development Process
Tools emerge from two sources, consistent with the two-path structure described in Section 2.7: the founder’s own workflow friction (building tools to solve real problems in advisory practice) and the content creation process (articles that naturally suggest a tool application). The development cycle:
Identify friction: A recurring workflow problem in the founder’s practice or a frequently asked question from the audience.
Build for self: Create a Claude skill that solves the problem for the founder’s own use. Test extensively in real project contexts.
Validate through content: Write an article explaining the framework behind the tool. Gauge audience interest through engagement metrics.
Productize: If engagement validates interest, develop the web app version alongside the Claude skill. Set pricing. Create documentation.
Launch: Release both formats. Link from relevant articles. Announce to Substack audience.
This sequence ensures every tool is battle-tested in real advisory work before it reaches the market. The founder is always the first user.

7.5 Website Build
Substack handles subscriptions and free distribution well but is a poor storefront for digests, box sets, books, and tools. A dedicated website with proper commerce functionality is needed to support the paid product catalog. Two primary paths are under consideration, plus a third worth evaluating:
Path A: Squarespace (Templated)
A templated UX with a clear shopping grid and built-in payment processing, with AI assistance for setup and content population. Fastest path to a live storefront with the least ongoing maintenance. Tradeoffs: design is constrained by the template, and Squarespace’s commerce fees stack on top of payment processor fees.
Path B: Custom HTML + Shopify Backend
A fully custom, AI-built front end with a Shopify backend for payment processing and commerce infrastructure. Full design control and access to Shopify’s excellent inventory, discount code, and digital product delivery tools. Tradeoffs: two platforms to maintain, and a full Shopify storefront may be more infrastructure than the catalog size currently warrants.
Path C: Webflow + Shopify Buy Button (Under Consideration)
Custom design control comparable to hand-coded HTML, but with visual tooling and a built-in CMS, paired with Shopify’s Buy Button embedded directly into product pages rather than running a full Shopify storefront. Lower monthly cost and less platform complexity than Path B while retaining most of its design flexibility.
Decision Criteria
The choice between paths should be made based on:
Time available for setup and ongoing maintenance versus desire for design control
Expected SKU volume—a small catalog (5–15 tools, a handful of digests) may not need full Shopify infrastructure
Total cost of platform fees plus payment processing fees across each path
Whether the website needs to support digital product delivery (tools, ebooks) natively or through a third-party fulfillment integration

8.1 Continuing Education: Courses and Curricula
Continuing education is adapted from top-performing Substack content and serves both AIA continuing education requirements and broader professional development needs across the OAC disciplines. This vertical has two distinct levels that should not be conflated:
Single CE Courses
Individual continuing education courses, typically 1–2 credit hours, adapted directly from a single high-performing article or a tightly related cluster of articles. Larger Curricula
Multi-session programs or certificate-level offerings that synthesize multiple OAC zones into a structured learning path—closer in scope to a short course than a single CE credit. Appropriate venues include university extension programs, AIA component-level certificate programs, and ULI’s educational initiatives.
Delivery Format
Both single courses and larger curricula should be designed for dual delivery, consistent with the platform’s approach to tools:
Web/self-paced: Recorded or written modules delivered online, asynchronous, lower marginal cost per additional student
Print/live: Workshop-style delivery with printed materials, appropriate for in-person AIA chapter events, conference pre-sessions, or university guest lectures

11.1 Assumptions to Validate
Audience growth: 1,000–3,000 subscribers in year 1 from cold start. Dependent on LinkedIn and speaking.
Conversion rate: 1–3% of free subscribers purchasing paid products (industry standard, unvalidated for this niche).
Content pace: 2 articles/week as a side project, sustainable long-term—increasingly validated in practice rather than purely speculative.
Engagement tier pricing: The low-cost threshold needed to keep this tier a flywheel accelerant rather than a revenue center has not yet been set or tested.
Tool demand: AEC professionals will pay for OAC-specific AI tools (unvalidated).
Tool pricing: $25–$500 range is appropriate for this market (needs competitive analysis).
Advisory pipeline: Content-to-advisory funnel exists but conversion timeline is unknown.
Visual content cost: Color printing implications for POD.
Analytics access: Manual export is reliable path; API access may come later.
Format pattern effectiveness: Initial taxonomy borrowed from Ouyang; must be validated for OAC audience.
Speaking fee ranges: Preliminary estimates need validation against comparable AEC industry speakers.
11.2 Key Risks
Writing cadence or quality drop: The single point of failure for the entire flywheel (Section 2.6). Currently well-managed but requires ongoing vigilance disproportionate to its apparent ease of execution.
Paid engagement tier mission drift: If the tier drifts toward revenue maximization, it stops accelerating the flywheel and becomes a barrier to advisory relationships (Section 2.4, 2.6).
Audience acquisition: Cold start with minimal budget. Mitigation: speaking + LinkedIn consistency.
Time constraint: Full-time role limits capacity. Tool development competes with content. Mitigation: batch, buffer, AI assistance.
Market saturation: AEC content is competitive. Mitigation: cross-disciplinary positioning + tools are genuinely unique.
Revenue timing: $100K+ by year 2 unlikely from cold start. Mitigation: advisory revenue bridges gap.
Platform risk: Substack dependency, including for the engagement tier’s platform-leverage mechanics. Mitigation: build email list; content and tools are portable; monitor Substack platform changes that could affect subscriber badge/rising-list dynamics.
Tool maintenance: One-time purchase model limits revenue per user. Tools may require updates as regulations, codes, or standards change. Mitigation: version updates as new purchases, not free patches.
Craft analysis over-optimization: Chasing format patterns at expense of authentic voice. Mitigation: patterns are tools, not templates.
